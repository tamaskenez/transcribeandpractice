⏺ Your calculation is correct. The negative slope is the expected result. What's
  different is the meaning: the time derivative gives you a frequency offset
  relative to the bin, but the frequency derivative gives you an absolute time.

  Why

  With a fixed FFT origin, take one complex component x(n) = e^{jω₀n} and a
  window centred at c:

  X(c, ω) = Σ x(n) w(n−c) e^{−jωn}
          = e^{j(ω₀−ω)c} · W(ω−ω₀)      (W is real and positive for a symmetric
  Gaussian)

  So the phase is φ(c, ω) = (ω₀ − ω)·c.

  - Along time: ∂φ/∂c = ω₀ − ω. This is the offset from the bin frequency to the
    true frequency. It's zero at f, positive below and negative above, which is
    your line 46.
  - Along frequency: ∂φ/∂ω = −c. This isn't an offset. It's minus the absolute
    time position of the energy, measured from the FFT origin (sample 0). This
    is the group delay.

  In the stable part of the signal the energy under the window sits at the
  window centre c. So ∂φ/∂ω = −c, which falls linearly as the frame advances: a
  negative slope. In your units (one bin is 1 Hz, so Δω = 2π/48000 per bin, and
  the column index j gives c = 999 + j):

  d_arg_M_df(kf, j) ≈ −2π·(999 + j)/48000

  That's a slope of about −1.3e-4 rad per frame, going from about −0.13 to −0.52
  rad.

  The flat parts at the start and end

  These are reassignment doing its job. The tone starts at n₀ ≈ 1667. While the
  window centre c is still before n₀, the only energy the window sees is at or
  just after n₀. So the energy's time centroid stays near n₀ while c keeps
  moving, and the curve goes flat. The same happens at the end, near 3333. Every
  frame overlapping the onset gets pulled back to the onset, which is how
  reassignment sharpens transients. Further out, where only noise remains, the
  values are just noise.

  How to plot the reassigned time

  You chose the fixed origin, which is fine. The reassigned time and the time
  correction are:

  Nfft  = fs;                                   % 48000, so 1 bin = 2π/Nfft rad
  t_hat = -d_arg_M_df * Nfft / (2*pi);          % reassigned time in samples
  (absolute)
  cc    = cs(1:end);                            % frame centres
  dt    = t_hat - cc;                           % correction: ~0 in steady state

  figure(5);
  plot(cs, t_hat(kf,:), cs, cs), grid;          % t_hat follows c, then flattens
  at onset/offset

  t_hat should run along the diagonal t = c in the stable part and sit flat near
  1667 and 3333 at the edges. dt is close to 0 in the middle and shows the
  corrections at the edges. That makes it the true counterpart of what you plot
  on line 46.

  If you move the phase origin to each frame's centre, the two cases swap. The
  phase becomes ω₀·c, so ∂φ/∂c = ω₀ gives the absolute frequency, and ∂φ/∂ω = 0
  gives the time offset relative to c. Many papers use that convention, which is
  why the two derivatives look asymmetric with a fixed origin.

  A small off-by-one

  Row k of M is frequency k−1 Hz, so kf = 500 is 499 Hz and the 500 Hz bin is
  M(501, :). It barely matters here, since one bin is only 1 Hz and the Gaussian
  main lobe is much wider. For the same reason, d_arg_M_df(k) is the difference
  between bins k+1 and k.

❯ Now I understand, thank you. Now I'm thinking about how this thing would look
  like if instead of STFT I get a filterbank. To make things simpler, let's
  filter an analytic signal back and forth to get zero phase. I think in this
  case we need to use the other convention, phase origin moves with frame
  centre. The derivative of the angle across time gives us the absolute
  (instantanous) frequency. Then I'd expect the derivative of angle across
  frequency will be zero when the signal is stable, and the sign of the
  derivative reflects whether the signal is increasing or decreasing, is this
  correct?

⏺ Yes, mostly. The zero-phase analytic filterbank is the moving-origin STFT. The
  one thing to refine is "increasing or decreasing": the sign tells you whether
  the energy at that bin lies ahead of or behind the current time.

  Why the filterbank is the moving-origin STFT

  Take a zero-phase complex bandpass h_k(m) = g(m)·e^{jω_k m}, where g is real
  and symmetric:

  y_k(n) = Σ_m x(m) g(n−m) e^{jω_k(n−m)}
         = e^{jω_k n} · X_fixed(n, ω_k)

  The factor e^{jω_k n} moves the phase origin to the frame centre n. With the
  earlier result φ_fixed = (ω₀ − ω)·n for a stable tone:

  - Along time: ∂φ/∂n = ω_k + (ω₀ − ω_k) = ω₀. This is the absolute
    instantaneous frequency, as you expected.
  - Along frequency: ∂φ/∂ω = n − t̂, where t̂ is the time centroid of the energy.
    This is the time offset relative to the frame centre, so it's 0 for a stable
    signal. ✔

  What the sign means

  The reassigned time is t̂ = n − ∂φ/∂ω.

  - Before an onset: the energy is ahead of n, so ∂φ/∂ω < 0.
  - After an offset: the energy is behind n, so ∂φ/∂ω > 0.

  So it isn't about the raw signal envelope going up or down. It tells you where
  the energy is relative to the window centre.

  For a Gaussian window, your intuition does hold exactly. The STFT phase and
  log-magnitude are then linked like the real and imaginary parts of an analytic
  function. With σ the window's standard deviation in samples and ω in
  rad/sample:

  t̂ − n   = −∂φ/∂ω = σ² · ∂ ln|X| / ∂n
  ω̂ − ω_k =          (1/σ²) · ∂ ln|X| / ∂ω

  So the sign of ∂φ/∂ω is minus the sign of how fast that bin's magnitude
  changes over time:

  - If the magnitude rises, ∂φ/∂ω < 0.
  - If it falls, ∂φ/∂ω > 0.

  This covers onsets, offsets, and also a chirp sweeping through the bin: the
  magnitude rises as the chirp approaches, so the energy is still to come. For
  non-Gaussian windows the relation is only approximate. As a check, you can
  compute both sides in your script and compare them.

  Practical caveats

  1. Forward and backward filtering with a complex filter. The backward pass
     applies h(−m), which has frequency response H(−ω). That passband sits at
     −ω_k, so the forward and backward passes would nearly cancel. The backward
     pass must use conj(h), which gives conj(H(ω)) and a total response of |H|².
     I don't know whether MATLAB's filtfilt conjugates complex coefficients; it
     may not accept them at all, so check that. If you do the passes yourself:
  y = filter(b, a, xa);                     % forward
  y = flipud(filter(conj(b), conj(a), flipud(y)));   % backward with conj
     A simpler alternative is to heterodyne. Multiply by e^{−jω_k n}, apply a
     real zero-phase lowpass with filtfilt, then multiply by e^{jω_k n}. This is
     exactly the moving-origin STFT, and the effective window is the lowpass's
     autocorrelation.
  2. Effective window. With forward-backward filtering, the effective window is
     g ⋆ g: symmetric, with a real non-negative spectrum |G|². That's good. But
     it's only Gaussian if g is close to Gaussian, which matters for the
     identity above.
  3. Derivative across channels. ∂φ/∂ω from differences between neighbouring
     channels only means "group delay of one window" if all channels share the
     same g and are closely spaced. You also have to divide by the actual Δω
     between channels. With a constant-Q bank (different σ per channel), the
     difference mixes different windows. In that case, compute it per channel
     with the auxiliary filters t·h(t) and h'(t) (Auger & Flandrin's method).
     This gives t̂ and ω̂ with no cross-channel differences:
  t̂ = n  − Re{ Y_th / Y_h }        ω̂ = ω_k + Im{ Y_dh / Y_h }   (signs depend on
  convention)
     One nice side effect of the moving-origin convention: across channels the
     phase is flat for stable parts, so unwrapping across frequency is trivial.

❯ I know the Auger & Flandrin's method but I can't use it with an IIR filter,
  can I?

⏺ Yes, you can. Auger & Flandrin only need the outputs of the filters t·h(t) and
  h'(t). For a rational (IIR) h, both of those are rational too, so they can be
  built as IIR filters. Forward-backward filtering only adds one product-rule
  step.

  The derivative filter h' is the easy one

  x * h' = (x * h)' = y'. So ω̂ = Im(y'/y) is just the phase derivative of the
  output, which you already compute from consecutive samples. You don't need an
  extra filter for it.

  The time-weighted filter n·h[n] is IIR

  In the z-domain, n·h[n] ↔ −z·dH/dz. Write w = z⁻¹ and H = B(w)/A(w). Then:

  n·h[n]  ↔  w·(B'A − BA') / A²

  It has the same poles as h, each now doubled, and it's stable whenever H is.
  In MATLAB (with b and a as coefficient vectors in powers of z⁻¹):

  function [bt, at] = time_weighted(b, a)
  % filter whose impulse response is n.*h[n], for h = impz(b,a)
  n = max(numel(b), numel(a));
  b = [b(:).' zeros(1, n-numel(b))];  a = [a(:).' zeros(1, n-numel(a))];
  db = (1:n-1) .* b(2:end);  da = (1:n-1) .* a(2:end);
  bt = [0, conv(db, a) - conv(b, da)];
  at = conv(a, a);
  end

  Check with one pole, b = 1, a = [1 −p]: the result is p·z⁻¹/(1 − p z⁻¹)²,
  which is the transform of n·pⁿ. ✓

  For high-order or high-Q filters, squaring A as one polynomial is numerically
  poor. Work per section instead, using the product rule:

  n·(h₁*h₂) = (n·h₁)*h₂ + h₁*(n·h₂)

  Gammatone-like case (a cascade of N identical complex one-pole stages 1/(1 − p
  z⁻¹)): this one is especially cheap. There h_N[n] = C(n+N−1, N−1)·pⁿ, and
  therefore:

  n·h_N[n] = N·(h_{N+1}[n] − h_N[n])

  So Y_th = N·(Y_{N+1} − Y_N), and the only extra cost is one more one-pole
  stage.

  With forward-backward (zero-phase) filtering

  The effective response is h_eff = h * h̃, where h̃[n] = conj(h[−n]) is the
  backward pass. Applying the product rule, and using n·h̃ = −(n·h)~:

  n·h_eff = (n·h) * h̃  −  h * (n·h)~

  That takes four filter runs:

  yf  = filter(b,  a,  xa);              % forward h
  ytf = filter(bt, at, xa);              % forward n·h
  bwd = @(bb,aa,v) flipud(filter(conj(bb), conj(aa), flipud(v)));

  y   = bwd(b,  a,  yf);                 % h * h~       -> the zero-phase output
  yt  = bwd(b,  a,  ytf) - bwd(bt, at, yf);   % n·h_eff

  t_hat = (0:numel(y)-1).' - real(yt ./ y);   % reassigned time, absolute
  samples
  w_hat = diff(unwrap(angle(y)));             % reassigned (instantaneous)
  frequency

  Sign check with an impulse at t₀: y = h_eff(n − t₀) and yt = (n − t₀)·h_eff(n
  − t₀), so t̂ = t₀. ✓

  Caveats

  - Unlike the Gaussian case, the time centroid of h_eff isn't tied to how the
    magnitude changes, so t̂ isn't the same as σ²·∂ ln|Y|/∂n. That doesn't
    matter, because the method doesn't depend on that identity.
  - Reassignment works best when h_eff is unimodal and mostly positive, as it is
    for gammatone or Butterworth-like bandpasses. If it has strong ringing or
    sidelobes, the centroid gets less meaningful.
  - Forward-backward filtering is offline only. A causal real-time version would
    keep just the forward pass, so the time origin of h and the phase origin go
    back to being your choice.

  the one-pole case and the impulse sign by hand.

❯ That's great. Now, can we somehow combine the time offset we get from the
  reassignment along time and the derivative of the instantanous amplitude?
  What I'm thinking if we model the signal locally as an exponentially
  increasing or decaying sinusoid, we can compute the beta exp(beta * t) from
  both the time reassignment offset and the derivative and magnitude of the
  instantaneous amplitude. If the two estimatation are the same we know that
  locally the exponential decay is a good model, indeed. If they differ we can
  draw conclusions from the difference. Would this work?

⏺ It works, but not quite in the form you describe. The result depends on the
  window. With a Gaussian window the two estimates are always identical, for any
  signal, so comparing them tells you nothing. With an IIR window they differ
  even for a perfect exponential, so you have to compare against a predicted
  value, and the leftover difference is hard to interpret.

  The exponential model through a filter

  Take x(n) = e^{s₀n} with s₀ = β + jω₀. Then for any filter:

  y(n) = Σ x(n−m) h(m) = e^{s₀n} · H(s₀),     H(s) = Σ h(m) e^{−sm}

  The output is the input scaled by H evaluated at a complex frequency. That
  gives two estimates.

  1. From the amplitude, independent of the window:
  y'/y = s₀   →   β = Re(y'/y) = d ln|y|/dn,   ω̂ = Im(y'/y)
     For this model it's exact in discrete time too: diff(log(abs(y))) = β.
  2. From the time-weighted filter:
  yt/y = Σ m h(m) e^{−s₀m} / H(s₀) = −(d/ds) ln H(s) |_{s₀}
  t̂ − n = −Re(yt/y) = Re( d ln H/ds |_{s₀} )
     This depends on the window shape and on the frequency offset Δω = ω₀ − ω_k.

  Gaussian window: always equal, so no information

  For g = e^{−m²/2σ²}, ln G(s) = σ²s²/2, so t̂ − n = Re(σ² s₀) = σ²β. That
  matches the amplitude estimate, and it doesn't depend on Δω.

  But this isn't a confirmation of the model. A Gaussian satisfies g' =
  −(t/σ²)·g, so for every input:

  yt/y = σ² (jω_k − y'/y)

  That's the identity from earlier (t̂ − n = σ²·∂ln|Y|/∂n). The time-weighted
  filter is a linear combination of the derivative filter and h. Onsets, chirps,
  noise and interference from other components all leave the two β estimates
  equal.

  IIR window: they differ even for a perfect exponential

  Expand ln H in cumulants of h_eff. For a symmetric h_eff, the odd terms
  vanish:

  t̂ − n = κ₂·β + (κ₄/6)·(β³ − 3βΔω²) + …

  - κ₂ is the variance of the window.
  - κ₄ is its excess kurtosis term, which is zero for a Gaussian.

  So equating β_amp = (t̂−n)/κ₂ is wrong even when the model holds exactly. The
  correct test is:

  1. Estimate s₀ = y'/y.
  2. Predict yt/y = −d ln H/ds at s₀. Since H is rational, you can evaluate it
     in closed form at z₀ = e^{s₀}.
  3. Compare the prediction with the measured yt/y.

  That's one complex residual, so two real consistency checks. They're zero when
  the model holds and nonzero otherwise. The catches:

  - Weak sensitivity. The residual only comes from how non-Gaussian the window
    is (κ₄ and higher). Gammatone and Butterworth-like bandpasses are fairly
    Gaussian near their centre, so the residual is small compared with noise.
  - Ambiguity. Amplitude curvature, chirp rate, a neighbouring partial and noise
    all feed into the same two numbers. A nonzero residual says the model
    failed, but not how.

  Better ways to do what you want

  1. Compare windows instead of filters. Under the exponential model, s₀ = y'/y
     is exact for every window. Compute it from two filterbanks with different
     lengths, such as short and long gammatones. If they agree, the model holds
     over the longer span. If they disagree, something non-exponential is inside
     the long window (an onset, a beating partner, FM). This is easy to
     interpret and doesn't depend on any Gaussian identity.
  2. Mixed partial derivative ∂²φ/∂n∂ω. Nelson and Fulop & Fitz use it as a
     local test of whether energy behaves like a sinusoid or an impulse (near 0
     for a sinusoid, near 1 in magnitude for an impulse), for deciding which
     reassigned points to keep.
  3. Fit a richer local model directly. The distribution derivative method
     (Hamilton & Depalle) and the generalized derivative method (Marchand &
     Depalle) estimate polynomial log-amplitude and phase parameters exactly,
     with β and chirp rate solved jointly. Second-order synchrosqueezing
     (Oberlin, Meignen & Perrier) does the same for the chirp rate. These
     methods use more auxiliary filters (t²h, h'') to get independent equations,
     rather than consistency checks that turn out to be identities.
